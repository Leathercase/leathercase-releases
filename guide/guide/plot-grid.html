Plot grid ​

The plot grid shows what every thread of the story is doing in every scene. A scene is a row, a thread is a column, and a cell holds what the thread does in that scene, in your own words. You type into the grid the way you type into a spreadsheet, and every cell you write becomes one line of a plain markdown file, StoryThreads.md, that you can open and edit without Leathercase. The grid never writes into your chapters: it keeps its own file of rows, PlotGrid.md, made from the story the first time it is read.

Opening the grid ​

Open a story, then pick Plot grid under the story's name in the sidebar, press Ctrl+4 (Cmd+4 on macOS), or type "Plot grid" in the command palette (Ctrl/Cmd+K).

If the story has no scenes and no threads yet, the grid says so: "No scenes yet. A row is a scene, and scenes come from headings in your chapter notes. Write the headings here first, start from a shape someone has already worked out, or write a chapter in your own software." It offers two ways in, New scene and Start from a template…, and a link to How this story is read for when you expected scenes and see none.

The grid's two files ​

The grid stands on two markdown files in the story's author data folder (for The Lamp at Ferrol, The Lamp at Ferrol/suite/). Both are yours to read and edit by hand.

FileWhat it holdsWho changes it
PlotGrid.mdThe rows: acts as # headings, chapters as ##, scenes as ###, a summary as a comment under a scene. Each row made from your story carries a hidden source: comment linking it to the scene it stands for. A row without one is a planned scene.Leathercase writes it from your story the first time; after that, only your actions in the grid change it.
StoryThreads.mdThe columns and cells: one ## heading per thread, one list line per scene it touches.Every cell you write in the grid; and you, by hand.

The first reading ​

The first time a story is opened, Leathercase writes PlotGrid.md as a mirror of it: each folder of prose becomes an act, each file read as the story's prose a chapter, each heading with prose under it a scene, whatever they are called. A file with no headings is one scene. Only files declared as the story's prose make rows; the cast, plans, research and files nothing claims yet never do (see How a story is read). A summary declared in a chapter's front matter (summary:, synopsis:, logline: or description:) becomes the row's summary. Planned scenes from an older Outline.md, in the story's folder or its suite/ folder, are carried in once. Outline.md is read only for this; Leathercase never writes it, and every planned scene from then on goes into PlotGrid.md. The strip under the grid says what happened, for example: "The grid is now its own file, PlotGrid.md: 20 scenes from the story, and 2 planned scenes carried in from the outline. The story's files are untouched."

From then on the grid is yours to arrange. A change in your chapters never rewrites it. When the story and the grid stop agreeing at a row, the row is marked and you decide; see When the story changes.

The threads file ​

StoryThreads.md reads like an outline:

markdown
## Subplot: The ledger
- [[01 The Night the Lamp Went Out#The tower]] — plant: "One prism was cracked." the page left to be found
- [[06 What the Ledger Said#The vestry]] — payoff: the entry read in the mirror

The link names the scene (file and heading). After the dash comes an optional role (plant:), an optional word from the thread's scale, an optional quoted sentence from the scene as its anchor, and then your note. A planned scene is linked as [[PlotGrid#Storm]]. If the story's folder already holds a Story threads.md from Creative Writer at its root (the sample's Lamp at Ferrol does), Leathercase reads it as the starting point until its own file exists; the first change writes suite/StoryThreads.md and leaves the old file as it was.

Reading the grid ​

Rows ​

Rows are scenes, in the order of PlotGrid.md. With Group by chapter on (the default, in the side column's Rows section), they sit under bands:

  • An act band carries a folder icon and the act's name. A band over rows that come straight from a folder, not from the grid file, is marked folder.
  • A chapter band carries the chapter's name and its counts, such as "· 2 scenes · 1,240 words". A chapter of one scene with the same name is drawn as that scene's row alone.

Each row begins with the Scene cell:

  • A thin chip for the scene's point of view, so the eye's owner survives a sideways scroll. It is drawn in the colour of characters (or of places, when the POV column names a place), not in a colour of the character's own, and is grey when the scene has no POV.
  • The scene's name. Click it to open the scene in your writing software, at its heading where the software can go to a line. Double-click it to rename the row. A planned scene's name is greyed and does not open anything.
  • A line under the name: the word count; planned for a scene with no prose, a heading you wrote with nothing under it yet included; from the old outline for a planned scene carried in from Outline.md; or a change mark (new in the story, gone from the story, renamed in the story).
  • ↕ when you moved the row away from its place in the story's order, and ≠ where the gauge's lanes disagree (see The gauge).
  • A ⋯ row menu, and an info button on a changed row.

Then the Plot cell: the scene's summary, or, faint, its first sentence until a summary is written. A planned row with neither shows summary…. Double-click to write the summary.

Columns ​

After Scene and Plot come your threads, one per ## heading of StoryThreads.md. The heading's prefix gives the column its kind, and a coloured stripe on the header repeats it:

HeadingKindWhat it follows
Arc: [[Nora Vidal]]ArcOne character. The header carries the character's dot.
Theme: What do we owe the drowned?ThemeAn argument the book makes.
Subplot: The ledgerSubplotA line of events.
Kell's tugFree threadAnything else.

A prefix that is almost one of these, such as Arcs: or Sub-plot:, is read as a free thread, and a line over the grid says so: 'A heading starts with "Arcs:", which is not Arc, Theme or Subplot; it is read as a plain thread.'

Columns sit in blocks: Derived (the columns with a job, below), then Arcs, Themes, Subplots and Free. Each block's first header carries a small eyebrow with its name and count, such as Arcs · 1. Click the eyebrow to fold the block into one narrow column, with a tick in each row where any of its columns has a stop; click again to unfold.

A column's header shows its name (double-click to rename), a line of counts such as "4 of 20 · 4 verified · scale of 5", the theme's owner when it has one, and the column's summary under it. A comment line right under the heading in StoryThreads.md is that summary. Double-click it, or the faint summary… under a column that has none, to write it in place: Enter saves, Shift+Enter makes a new line, Esc puts it back, and an emptied field takes the line out.

Scene and Plot stay put while the columns scroll, and so does the header row.

Four columns with a job ​

A column can be given one of four jobs: POV, Time, Plot point or Main theme. Use the column's menu, Use as POV and so on; the job is ticked there, and choosing it again takes it off. The job is kept in the story's declaration, not in your files. When you declare a story whose project note has plot-pov, plot-time, plot-beats or plot-theme in its front matter, Leathercase takes the jobs from there, as it does for The Lamp at Ferrol.

  • POV, Time and Plot point move to the Derived block after Plot, and their header says job: POV and so on.
  • The Main theme is marked main and is read in a line over the grid: Main theme, the question, and how many scenes carry it. Themes owned by a character are listed beside it, "from Nora Vidal: …".
  • The Plot point says which beat a scene is at. A template with beats fills it when its rows are applied.

Nothing is inferred from a name: a column called "Time" that has not been given the job is an ordinary thread.

Cells ​

A cell shows the thread's stop at the scene: the note, or the quoted sentence when there is no note. Before it may come:

  • A glyph for the stop's role. Every thread has four roles: plant ▶, touch (no glyph), payoff ◀, reversal ▼. An arc has four more: want ▸, lie ▹, turn ▼, truth ◂.
  • A pill with the stop's state on the column's scale and where it sits, such as Doubt +3 or Blind faith -8. The sign is written, so colour is never alone; the pill's colour deepens the further from zero the stop sits. A number that does not fall in the state it names is dashed and marked !, and the gauge leaves it out.

+1 after the text says the thread has another stop at the same scene. An empty cell shows +.

A stop's anchor is checked against the prose every time the story is read:

StateMeaning
planA stop with no quote: a claim, typed ahead of the draft or from memory of it.
verifiedA stop whose quote Leathercase found in the scene.
brokenA stop whose quote is no longer in the scene: the prose moved. The cell says anchor no longer in the prose.

Present, unmoved ​

Turn on Present, unmoved in the Rows section and an arc column marks the scenes where its character is on the page and the arc has no stop, faintly: present, unmoved. It is the scene where someone is in the room and nothing happens to them. A column earns the mark only once it holds a verified stop, because on a first draft it would fire everywhere.

Audit view ​

Press V, or turn on Audit view in the Rows section, to draw every cell by its state: ◇ plan, ◆ verified, ◈ broken. Headers show the verified count even when it is zero, and the state line reads "40 cells · 12 filled" instead of "12 of 40 filled". It is a lens over the same grid and is not remembered.

The state line ​

The line at the top counts what the grid shows, for example 20 scenes · 2 planned · 6 columns · 34 of 120 filled · 30 verified · 1 broken · 1 hidden · 2 gaps. When columns are hidden or blocks folded, a reset link beside it shows them all again.

Writing in a cell ​

Click a cell to pick it; a thin ring marks it. The arrow keys move from there; Home and End go to the row's first and last column, Page Up and Page Down ten rows. With nothing picked, the first arrow lands on the first cell.

To write, press Enter on the picked cell or double-click it. The cell becomes a field with a thicker ring, as a spreadsheet rings the cell being edited, and the strip says only "Editing The ledger at The tower · enter saves · shift+enter a new line · esc puts the line back": Undo and Redo leave it until the cell is closed.

  • Enter saves. Leaving the field saves too, and so does switching view with a key while you type.
  • Shift+Enter makes a new line.
  • Esc puts the line back as it was.
  • Emptying the field and saving removes the stop.

The text you type is read the way StoryThreads.md is read: a leading role word and colon (payoff:) sets the role, a state and its number and a colon (Doubt 3:, or either one alone) set where the stop sits on the scale, and a sentence in quotes becomes the anchor. What you do not type keeps the stop's own: an existing role, word and quote ride above the field as tokens and stay. On a column with a scale, the scale's words sit under the field as chips; click one to put it in front of the note, or type the start of a word and press Tab to complete it. Tab is otherwise never taken.

The line lands in StoryThreads.md at once. Writing "she comes back" at The return in the scene Two writes - [[Novel#Two]] — she comes back.

To remove a stop, pick the cell and press Delete (or Backspace).

The side column ​

The side column (toggle it with Ctrl/Cmd+B or the button at the right of the head) holds sections you can open and close. Each shows its value in its title, so it reads without opening.

Cell ​

With nothing picked it says "Pick a cell to write what the thread does in that scene, or press Enter on it to write in place." With a cell picked, its title shows the state (plan, verified, broken, empty or editing), and the section edits the same line the cell does:

  • A crumb naming the file, the scene, the column and its kind, with open scene o to open the scene in your software.
  • Role: the four thread roles, or the eight arc roles under an arc.
  • State on the scale and Place, its number on the axis, on a column with a scale (none and an empty place to clear them); otherwise the cell's State.
  • A sentence of the scene that marks it, the anchor, "quoted as written". Pick… (or " on the grid) lists the scene's sentences; on a broken stop the sentences nearest the lost quote come first. Clicking one writes it as the anchor straight away. With no prose the list says "No prose to pick from yet."
  • What the thread does here, the note. While you type in the cell itself, this field follows.
  • A line with the state in full, such as "verified · the quote is in the scene".
  • Save stop writes the line, Cancel puts the selection away, and Remove stop takes the line out.

Repairing a broken anchor takes three keys: N walks to the next broken anchor, " lists the sentences, and a click picks one. P walks back.

Columns ​

The title reads "StoryThreads.md · 6", with the number hidden if any. Every column is listed under its block with its count ("4 of 20") or its job, and an eye to hide or show it. A hidden column stays hidden, and the state line counts it. What is hidden is kept in GridLayout.json in the story's suite/ folder, so it travels with the story to every computer that opens it.

Under the list:

  • New column: type a heading and click Add a column. Write Arc: [[Nora Vidal]], Theme: …, Subplot: … or a plain name. It is written to StoryThreads.md as an empty section, ready to fill.
  • From a template… lists the columns the templates offer that the grid does not have yet, grouped by template, with a placeholder arc spelled out once per character. Picking one adds it; a column the template gives a job takes that job.
  • Start from a template… opens the template sheet; see Templates.

Rows ​

The title counts the rows and the planned ones. It holds three switches, Group by chapter, Audit view (v) and Present, unmoved, and a field for a scene not written yet: type a title ("A scene not written yet") and click Plan a scene, or leave it empty to plan "New scene".

Belongs to ​

A theme can be the story's own question or a character's, and a free column can be one more pressure on a character, with its own scale beside their arc: Mina Harker's purity beside her agency. Belongs to… opens a sheet that explains the difference ("A character theme is the question that character is asking, with a scale of their answers. It is not the arc"), offers an Owner list (The story's own question, then the cast) and an Against list (Nobody: their own gauge, then the cast), and shows The heading it writes, such as ## Theme: Is winning worth it? · [[Bastian Kell]] or ## Purity · [[Mina Harker]]. Only the heading changes; the scale and the stops stay. Save writes it.

Given to two characters, a column is one force between them, a tug of war: ## Trust · [[Clarice Starling]] · [[Hannibal Lecter]]. The first character pulls towards the negative end, the second towards the positive, and the sheet says so. The lane names both, under its scale: ◂ Clarice Starling · Hannibal Lecter ▸. A pair's gauge counts on both characters' sheets.

The main theme belongs to the story. If its heading names an owner, a line over the grid asks you to take the owner off or choose another main theme.

Every row the grid file holds has a ⋯ row menu. Each item writes PlotGrid.md with Undo; none touches your chapters.

Act and chapter bands have their own menu: New scene in this chapter (chapters only), New chapter below or New act, Rename…, Move up, Move down, and Delete chapter or Delete act. These act on the grid file, never on your folders.

The grid's ⋯ menu (Grid menu) has New scene, New chapter and New act at the end of the grid, plus Start from a template…, Save as template…, Show the gauge or Hide the gauge, and Keep a snapshot.

Linking a planned row to a written scene ​

When you write a scene you had planned, the grid offers to join them. This is a scene I wrote… opens "Which scene of the story is Storm?", listing the story's scenes the grid does not hold yet with their files. Pick one and the row takes the scene's words and anchors; its title and summary stay yours, and the stops written on the planned row move to the scene.

When the story changes ​

The grid never follows your files by itself. When they stop agreeing at a row, the row is marked and an info button opens a dialog that says why and asks what to do. Decide later closes it and leaves the mark.

MarkDialogChoices
new in the story"The Lens is new in the story. The scene is in Act III - Light/08 The Lens.md but not in the grid. The grid never changes by itself: choose what it becomes."Put it in the grid where the story's order puts it (or "…in a new chapter …" when its file is new); It is the planned scene … for each planned row it may be; Leave it out of the grid; and Not a scene, which declares the file's folder (or the file) as holding notes of a kind or role, so it and later files there never enter the grid.
gone from the story"The row stood for … in …, which the story's files no longer have."Keep it as a planned scene, Remove the row.
renamed in the story"The row stood for The tower in … The story now has The stair in …, which no row stands for."Follow it: this row is that scene (its stops follow), Keep the row as a planned scene.

Settling the old outline ​

If an older Outline.md planned scenes for a chapter you have since written, they are carried into that chapter and marked from the old outline, and the chapter band carries an info button. It opens "The old outline also planned …": for each carried scene choose Keep as a planned scene, It became … (one of the written scenes, which takes its stops, and its summary if it has none), or Drop it, then Apply.

Templates ​

A template is a starting shape: rows, columns, or both, written as headings. Applying one never writes a word of prose and never calls a model.

Start from a template… (grid menu, empty state, or the Columns section) opens a sheet in the side column. It lists the built-in templates and then your own (marked yours), each with its counts:

Pick one, then:

  • Apply: Rows and columns, Rows only or Columns only (choices the template lacks are greyed).
  • Columns: a tick per column. A placeholder theme or subplot takes a name of yours; a placeholder arc (Character A) takes a character from the cast; "One arc per character" counts your cast.

Apply writes it all as one step with Undo. Applying Save the Cat with rows and columns plans fifteen scenes and adds the B story column.

Save as template ​

Save as template… opens a sheet: a Name ("Noir in five moves"), and under Includes, Columns, with their jobs and Rows, with their summaries and beats. "Stops and prose are not copied." Save writes a new file under Templates/ in the story's author data folder and says where; a taken name gets a number. Your templates appear in the template sheet and, when they have beats, on the beat board.

The gauge ​

Every scene turns on a value, and the gauge draws where the story turns. A column's scale is an axis you cut into states, from most negative to most positive, and each stop sits at a place on it: the state the character is in and how far into it. A turn is the move from one place to the next. A fall on the positive side is still a negative turn: from Knowing 7 back to Doubt 3 is a turn of −4, though both states are positive.

The scale is one comment line under the column's heading, written as a ruler: where the axis starts, then each state and the number it ends at, up to the axis's end. A stop gives its state and its number:

markdown
## Arc: [[Nora Vidal]]
<!-- scale: -10 · Blind faith · -5 · Fear · 0 · Doubt · 5 · Knowing · 10 -->
- [[01 The Night the Lamp Went Out#The tower]] — want: Blind faith -8: "The lamp had not gone out."
- [[06 What the Ledger Said#The vestry]] — turn: Knowing 7: "Pilot on watch," Nora said.
  • A number on a cut belongs to the state nearer zero: on this ruler -5 is Fear and 5 is Doubt. Zero, where it is a cut, is no state at all; to have a neutral state, give it a stretch round zero (-1 · Calm · 1).
  • A state named with no number sits at the middle of its stretch: Doubt: sits at 2.5. A number with no state takes the state it falls in.
  • A number that does not fall in the state it names (Doubt -3), or that is off the axis, is flagged as misnumbered and left out of the gauge. The gauge never guesses which of the two you meant.
  • A scene with two stops moves inside the scene, and closes on the last: the gauge reads them in the order their quotes come in the scene, or, when one has no quote, in the order the threads note lists them.
  • A scale of numbers alone, -10 · 10, names no state, for a story you would rather gauge as a numbers game.

The older way of writing a scale, a list of words in equal steps with the middle one neutral, still reads, and every stop on it sits where it always did. The Lamp at Ferrol's theme carries one:

markdown
## Theme: What do we owe the drowned?
<!-- scale: silence, doubt, duty, witness, light -->
- [[07 The Storm Rises#The steps]] — duty: "Then it is a candle," said Inés. the lamp lit without the guild

Setting a scale ​

Set scale… in the column's menu opens a sheet. It says which column it is for and how a scale works: where the axis starts, then each state with the number it ends at, so it reads as the ruler it writes. A new scale starts as four blank states from -10 to 10.

  • Starts at is the axis's first number. Each state has its word and ends at, the number its stretch ends at; the last one is the axis's end.
  • Add a state adds one at the positive end; × takes a state out. A scale has at most 11 states. Leave every word blank for a scale of numbers alone.
  • Beside each state, how many cells carry it, or unused.
  • A state renamed in place is a rename: the sheet says how many cells use it ("doubt → unease in 2 cells"), and saving rewrites them.
  • A list of words in equal steps opens as the same words two steps apart, from minus their count to plus it, in the same order and on the same side of zero: "These words were in equal steps…". Saving writes it as a ruler.
  • What the gauge would count previews the charged scenes, the misnumbered ones, the crossings and the inversions, and shows the exact line it will write. A scale that cannot be one says why: numbers that do not rise, a state left blank among named ones, a repeated state, a role word, too few or too many.
  • Save scale writes it with one Undo. Remove scale takes the line out.

A scale line the gauge cannot read is named in the Gauge section, "…; fix it with Set scale…".

Reading the lane ​

When the gauge is shown, its lanes stand at the right edge of the grid. A lane's header shows the column's name (or audience for the main theme, or the owner), the scale as a row of swatches from red to green, and under it every state with its stretch of the axis, such as -10…-5 Blind faith (on a list of words, its charge, such as -1 doubt). A state no scene is in yet is faint; point at it to read how many scenes carry it. Click the swatches or the states to open the scale. Last come the lane's counts: charged scenes, inversions or turns, crossings, misnumbered scenes.

Each row draws the scene's place as a bar from the centreline, rose to the left below zero, green to the right above (on a list of words, as pipes, one per step); a stop at zero is a grey tick, and a scene nobody has placed draws nothing. Where the character crosses into another state, the row names the state at its top left. A line walks down the whole lane, through a point in the middle of each row, however tall the rows grow:

  • Running total (the default) adds every place up and walks the story's cumulative balance: each scene read as a push. Where the total changes sign the row carries a diamond and the word inversion. When the total outgrows the lane, the header says "line: 1 step = 2".
  • Scene to scene walks each scene's own place, and marks a turn where the move changes direction. A scene that moves inside itself draws its earlier places as small points above the close. The line is dashed across a scene with no place.

How violent a turn is, the gauge leaves to the line: the steeper it moves from one scene to the next, the harder the turn. A flip at a stop with no quote draws its mark hollow: no line of the prose marks the turn. A misnumbered stop draws a ! and counts as misnumbered until you fix its number or its state.

The Gauge section ​

The section's title says no scale yet, off, or how many lanes are drawn. With no scaled column it says "Give a column a scale from its menu, Set scale…, and the gauge draws where the story turns." Otherwise:

  • Show gauge turns the lanes on or off (also Show the gauge / Hide the gauge in the grid menu). It is on until you turn it off.
  • The line: Running total ("where the story flips") or Scene to scene ("where the move turns"), for every lane. A lane can read its own way: the button under its scale, Running total or Scene to scene, switches that lane alone, since a theme often reads best as a running total and an arc scene to scene. While some lanes read their own way the section says how many; choosing here sets them all again.
  • Lanes · 1 of 3: a switch for each column with a scale. By default the main theme is the lane, else the first scaled column. Three lanes sit side by side at most; ticking a fourth unticks the one you ticked first.
  • What the gauge counted: per lane, the charged scenes, the misnumbered ones, and where it flips; how many flips no line marks; and, with two lanes or more, where they disagree, by pair ("audience vs Nora Vidal: 2").

Rows where two lanes carry opposite signs get ≠ on the scene, with the odd lane's initial when one stands apart. Signs only: scales of different lengths do not compare in size. N and P also walk to the flips no quote marks.

Gaps ​

The Gaps section lists what the grid can count for itself, with no model: a column with a stop in fewer than a quarter of the written scenes (once eight scenes are written), and a character on the page for three scenes or more in a row while their arc says nothing. Each reads, for example, "Kell's tug has a stop in 4 of 20 written scenes." or "Nora Vidal is on the page in 3 scenes in a row, The hall to The yard, and the arc has no stop there.", with Show, which picks the first cell it points at. The state line counts them. With none it says "Nothing the grid can point at". Findings, not judgements: what to do about them is yours.

Snapshots ​

Keep a snapshot in the grid menu keeps today's grid as machine data, a file PlotGrid 2026-10-06.json in the story's suite/snapshots/ folder; a second snapshot on the same day replaces the first. Once one exists, a row of tabs sits over the grid: Now, then one tab per snapshot, newest first, named by its day ("6 Oct"). A snapshot tab draws the grid as it stood, read only: chapter bands, scenes with their counts, each cell as text with ✓ for verified and ✗ for broken. The state line counts it and ends in read only. Two tabs apart are the outline against the draft.

The Snapshots section lists them with their counts; click a day to open its tab, again to go back to Now. With none it says "None yet. A snapshot keeps today's grid as machine data, to set beside a later one."

What else the grid writes ​

What the grid knows about a character, an event or a theme is copied into a note Leathercase keeps for it in the author data folder: an arc into Characters/Nora Vidal.md, a subplot into Events/The ledger.md, a theme into Notes/What do we owe the drowned.md. The copy sits between two suite:grid marks under the heading "From the plot grid"; everything outside the marks is yours. The POV column only names a character and writes nothing. StoryThreads.md stays the one source: change a stop there or in the grid, not in the copy. If the marks in a note are broken, or writing the section would touch your text, the grid leaves the note as it is and the strip names it. These notes are what the character sheet and the Graph read.

How the grid is laid out (hidden columns, folded blocks, the frozen column, the switches, the gauge's lanes and line) is kept in GridLayout.json in the story's suite/ folder. Column jobs and block order are kept in the story's declaration, Story.json. See Files Leathercase writes.

Undo ​

Under the grid runs a strip. Before anything is written it says "Click a cell and press Enter to write in it." After each write it names it, such as Wrote The ledger at The road, Planned a scene above The tower, Applied Save the Cat: 3 columns, 15 scenes or Set the scale of …: …, with Undo, the stack's depth ("ctrl+z · 3 in the stack"), and Redo when something was undone.

Ctrl/Cmd+Z undoes and Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+Z redoes, outside a text field. Undo puts the file back whole; a step that wrote two files (a template, a planned row linked to a scene) puts both back together. If a file changed outside Leathercase since, nothing is written and the strip says "The file changed outside Leathercase since; nothing was written, and the step was dropped." Enter and Delete on a button belong to the button, so pressing them on Undo never edits a cell.

Proposals from agents ​

An agent you connected with Lay out can propose planned rows in the chapter and part they belong to, a summary for each row, columns with their kind, a line about them and a scale, and cells rated on that scale with the sentence that shows them. With the assistant's layer on (Ctrl/Cmd+.), what it proposed shows dashed beside your own, until you accept or dismiss it. Review in Plot grid, on a changeset in the inbox, opens the grid with a review bar beside it.

A row title, a summary, a column or a cell you accepted from an agent is drawn on a light grey wash: generated. Point at it to read it whole and to see which agent laid it out. The mark goes the first time you edit the text, here or in another editor. See Proposals and the inbox.
