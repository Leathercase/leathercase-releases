Lenses and style checks ​

A lens is a reading pass laid over the manuscript page. It tints the words it is about (the length of each sentence, a tired phrase, who is speaking, a phrase you reached for twice) so you can see one kind of thing across the whole book. Only one lens is on at a time, because five highlights cannot be read and one can. Every lens runs offline, on this computer, over the prose as it stands. A lens points; you decide. None of them changes a word of your text.

Turning a lens on ​

On the manuscript page:

  • Open the Lens section of the side column and click a lens. The one in force has a filled circle ◉; the others an empty one ◌.
  • Press L to step to the next lens, round to No lens after the last.
  • Click the lens name in the footer at the bottom right (lens: rhythm) to step to the next.
  • Press Esc with nothing selected to turn the lens off.

The lenses, in the order L steps through them: No lens, Rhythm, Style checks, Dialogue, Echoes, Words, Accents.

With a lens on, select a paragraph (click a sentence, or use the arrow keys). What the lens found, under the lens list, then lists every mark the lens made in that paragraph, each with its note. When there is none it says Nothing found in this paragraph. Hover a tinted word on the page for the same note.

Rhythm ​

The rhythm lens tints each sentence by how long it feels to a reader, cool for short and warm for long, so the rhythm of a paragraph shows at a glance. It weighs more than the word count: commas and clauses add weight, and long words add weight, so a sentence of twelve words strung on commas can sit higher than twelve plain ones. Abbreviations such as "Mr." do not end a sentence. The tiers mean the same thing in every paragraph of the book.

What the lens found names each sentence's band, such as rhythm tier 2 of 6.

The number of bands is Rhythm tiers in Settings › Lenses, from 4 to 6 (6 by default).

The gutter's sentence meter, shown whatever the lens, is a plainer measure of the same thing: one tick per sentence, its width the sentence's length in words (see The gutter).

Style checks ​

The style lens points at the sentences you might look at again. Each kind of finding has its own tint and a note that says what it saw and what to try.

CheckWhat it points atExample note
clichéA tired phrase from Leathercase's list.Cliché: "at the end of the day". Say what you actually saw.
passiveA verb in the passive voice: "was written", "got hit". Names ("was Helen"), states ("was tired") and numbers ("was eleven") are left alone.Passive voice. Who is doing this? Put them in front of the verb.
filterA verb that puts the narrator between the reader and the scene: saw, heard, felt, noticed, realised, could see, found herself and the like. Speech, nouns ("the felt"), passives ("was decided") and idioms ("smelled of", "seemed to") are left alone.Filter verb. Render what was seen, not the seeing: "she saw the door open" → "the door opened".
adverbA manner adverb ending in -ly, where a stronger verb could do the work. One on a dialogue tag gets a sharper note. Words such as "suddenly" or "obviously", which no verb replaces, are left alone, as is anything inside speech.Adverb on a dialogue tag. Let the line itself carry the "quietly".
repetitionA word that comes back within thirty words, and a run of sentences that open with the same word (three in a row; four for "the", "a", "I", "it" and "there")."lamp" echoes a word 12 words back.
metaphorA possible figure of speech: a concrete word on an abstract noun ("velvet silence"), "X is a Y", or a dead metaphor. It asks; it does not judge.Possibly figurative: … Fresh, or familiar?
nominalizationAn action hiding in a noun after a light verb: "made a decision", "gave an explanation".Nominalization. "made … decision" → "decided".
weak verbA long sentence (fourteen words or more) whose only verb is "to be". Short ones such as "The sky was red." are fine.18 words carried by "was". Give the sentence a verb that does something.

The checks read English. Their word lists (clichés, filter verbs, adverbs to spare) and the grammar tagger behind them are English ones, so on a story in Portuguese or Spanish they say little that is useful.

Choosing which checks tint ​

Settings › Lenses › Style checks has a chip for each kind: adverb, passive, filter, cliché, repetition, metaphor, nominalization, weak verb. All are on by default. Click a chip to turn that kind off; off is off everywhere. The preference is kept on this machine.

Not here: keeping one finding out of a paragraph ​

When a finding is a choice you made, you can tell the lens so, in your own note. Put a hidden comment in the paragraph naming the kind:

markdown
At the end of the day, she left. %% not cliche %%

That paragraph then shows no cliché finding. The kinds are named cliche, passive, filter, adverb, repetition, metaphor, nominalization and weakverb. You type the comment in your own app; Leathercase never writes it. Obsidian hides %% … %% comments, and the manuscript export leaves them out.

Dialogue ​

The dialogue lens tints speech and thought, and finds who says each line of speech. It follows a conversation from one paragraph to the next.

What the lens found says, for each span, speech, Nora Vidal, thought, Nora Vidal, speech, speaker not found or thought.

How the speaker is found ​

Leathercase tries, in order:

  1. A pin of yours. A hidden comment with the character's name just before a line of speech, %% Tomás %% "Aye.", names the speaker for certain.
  2. A dialogue tag. "Go," Inés said.
  3. A name in the paragraph. A character named in the same paragraph, by name or alias.
  4. Turn-taking. In a back-and-forth between two voices, an untagged line goes to the one whose turn it is.

The first three are certain. Turn-taking is a guess: those lines are given a speaker too, and the accents lens and the character sheet treat them as guessed. A line no rule can place reads speech, speaker not found.

The cast the lens attributes speech to is your character notes, each with its name and its aliases:. The story's Speakers setting narrows and orders the cast and adds names that have no note (see Story settings).

The dialogue lens does not colour lines by speaker: every line of speech takes the same speech tint, every thought the thought tint, and the speaker is named in What the lens found in the side column. A character's colour, from color: (or colour:) in their note, or pinned after their name in Speakers as The Lamp at Ferrol does with Nora Vidal #1f5f7a, or else one from the palette in cast order, is what the accents lens marks that character's words in.

Speech and thought conventions ​

The lens has to know how your story marks speech and thought. The story's Prose settings say so:

SettingChoices
Speech is written asAs the prose reads, Double quotes (“Not tonight,” she said.), Single quotes (‘Not tonight,’ she said.), A dash (— Not tonight, she said.), No speech marks.
Thoughts are written asAs the prose reads, An italic paragraph (He is up there. He has to be.), Italics anywhere, Single quotes (‘He is up there,’ she thought.), Not marked.

As the prose reads, the default, lets Leathercase tell the convention from your paragraphs. Under it, a word or two in italics inside a sentence is emphasis, never a thought; a whole italic paragraph, or an italic sentence of three words or more, or one followed by "she thought" or "he wondered", is a thought. A dash at the start of a paragraph opens speech, and each further dash in it switches between narration and speech, as Portuguese and French prose do. Straight single quotes are told from apostrophes by where they sit.

The settings page shows which convention it read in your prose, and in how many paragraphs, with one of your own sentences re-marked in each other convention. See Story settings.

Echoes ​

The echoes lens hears what is hard to hear from inside a draft: a phrase you reached for in more than one scene, and a sentence written again in slightly different words a few scenes later. It reads the whole story, scene by scene.

What the lens found says "There was salt on the wind" recurs in 2 scenes for a phrase, or echoes a sentence 3 scenes away for a near-repeat.

Three things are never an echo:

  • A character's name, or any word of a name or an alias.
  • A phrase you quoted in a thread. A motif you kept on purpose is yours, not a finding.
  • Phrases too common to mean anything.

How close two passages must be before they are marked is Echo sensitivity in Settings › Lenses: Low, Medium (the default) or High. The same finder runs in the threads view.

Words ​

The words lens marks your own word lists: the words you overuse, the words you never want to use, the stage directions you lean on. Each list has its colour, and a marked word shows in it. What the lens found says the list and the word, such as Never use: suddenly.

The lists live in WordLists.md in the story's suite folder. With no list yet the lens says: No word list yet. Put one in WordLists.md in your suite folder: a heading per category, the words under it.

From the page you can:

  • Put a word in a list. With the words lens on, drag across a word or a short phrase (up to four words). Under the findings, Put “felt” in a word list appears with a List field (it suggests your lists, and Never use as a start) and Add. Type a list name that does not exist and the list is made.
  • Take a word out. Select a paragraph where the lens marked a word, and click Take “felt” out of the word lists under the findings.

Both write WordLists.md, never your chapter. The lists can also be edited on the story's settings page, and by hand. The file's format is on the Comments and word lists page.

Accents ​

The accents lens marks how each character speaks: the words that are theirs, and the words they never say. Each mark is in the character's colour: the color: in their note, the colour pinned after their name in the story's Speakers setting, or one from the palette (see Dialogue).

  • A word a character uses, in their own certain speech, is marked: Nora Vidal's word: “lamp”.
  • A word a character never says, found in their certain speech, is a slip: Nora Vidal never says “salvage”.
  • In a line only guessed to be theirs by turn-taking, the mark is dotted and never counts as a slip: Nora Vidal's word: “tower”, in a line only guessed to be theirs.

Words in the narration, or in another character's speech, are not marked.

A character's words come from two places, joined:

WhereWhat
The character's noteaccent: and accent-never: in its front matter, as Nora Vidal's note in the sample has accent: [tower, lamp, watch] and accent-never: [salvage]. Yours to edit, in your app.
Accents.mdIn the story's suite folder. Kept by Leathercase from what you tell it on the page or on the character's Voice tab.

Accents.md reads like this, a heading per character and two lines under it:

markdown
## Nora Vidal
says: aye, the lamp
never: okay, gonna

A word sits in one list per character. Saying a character never says a word they use moves it across.

With no accents for anyone yet the lens says: No accents yet. Give a character the words they use or never say on the Voice tab of their sheet, in Characters, or select a word in their speech. See Characters.

From the page, with the accents lens on, drag across a word or short phrase in a line of speech. Give “aye” to a character appears: Who offers the cast, with the paragraph's certain speaker first, and two buttons, Says “aye” and Never says “aye”. Either writes Accents.md.

The character sheet's Voice tab counts the same marks (marks in certain speech, more in guessed lines, slips) and has Open the accents lens, which opens this page with the lens on.

What the lenses never do ​

  • They never change your text, and never write into your notes.
  • They never send your text anywhere: every lens runs on this computer, with no model and no network.
  • They never read a screenplay: a Fountain story shows as a script with no lens over it.

The only files a lens writes are Leathercase's own WordLists.md and Accents.md, and only when you add or take out a word.
